Language Models Can Now Reason About and Rewrite Their Own Weights

Researchers demonstrate that a 27B model can diagnose errors and modify its parameter weights without gradients.

Traditional language models remain frozen after deployment, requiring complete retraining runs or expensive inference compute to improve performance. In a new study, researchers demonstrated self-rewriting, where a 27B model inspects its own weights, analyzes graded test failures, and directly rewrites its parameters without gradient descent or pre-training infrastructure. The model achieved a 6.6-point accuracy gain across 490 unseen benchmark problems on a single GPU. Crucially, the accuracy gains persisted across eight consecutive evaluation sessions, allowing the system to repair deficiencies and retain improvements permanently.

When Reasoning Models Think Too Much: Measuring Harmful Overthinking

New research identifies how extended chain-of-thought sequences frequently cause models to abandon correct answers.

When reasoning models continue evaluating problems after reaching correct answers, additional deliberation frequently hurts final performance. A new paper formalizes this phenomenon by contrasting a model's current commitment against its final verdict. Testing across MATH-500 and OlympiadBench, researchers observed DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B lose an average of 3.6 accuracy points on problems where it had already settled on the correct answer before continuing its chain. The research demonstrates that final-correctness probes cannot detect harmful drift, highlighting the necessity of calibrated early-stopping mechanisms during inference.

Premature or excessive reasoning can degrade accuracy, making stopping criteria vital for agent efficiency.

Mastering Finite Automata: Thompson’s Construction for Regular Expressions

A detailed breakdown shows how syntax patterns convert systematically into non-deterministic state machines.

Neso Academy's new Theory of Computation lecture revisits Thompson’s Construction Algorithm, the foundational method used to convert regular expressions into non-deterministic finite automata (NFA). By decomposing basic regular operations—concatenation, union, and Kleene star—into distinct state transitions with epsilon moves, the algorithm provides an elegant mechanical bridge between high-level text patterns and hardware-friendly execution models. For computer science students and software engineers, mastering this construction offers critical insight into how compiler front-ends and high-performance search engines process strings under the hood.

Understanding the algorithmic foundations of regex underpins modern lexical parsing, compiler design, and text engines.
