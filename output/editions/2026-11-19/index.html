Software Thermodynamics: Where Coding Agents Burn Tokens as Heat

Researchers apply classical thermodynamic principles to agentic software engineering, finding that roughly 80 percent of generated tokens represent computational waste.

A fascinating preprint introduces a thermodynamic theory of agentic coding, modeling token generation through classical concepts of work, entropy, and heat. By repeatedly prompting coding agents to apply and revert repository modifications against automated test suites—analogous to folding single molecules—the researchers defined software temperature based on sampling variance. The experiment discovered that approximately 80 percent of tokens produced in successful runs are expended on intermediate model calls that leave repository states unaltered, effectively acting as thermodynamic heat rather than productive computational work.

Treating token expenditures as thermodynamic energy provides a rigorous mathematical framework for optimizing expensive agentic code generation loops.

Digital Olfaction Startup Osmo Trains AI to Match Human Sniffers

By mapping chemical profiles to descriptive sensory notes, researchers in New Jersey are assembling a foundational model for scent.

In a New Jersey laboratory, biotechnology startup Osmo is pairing human sensory panels with chemical spectroscopy to construct the world's first foundation model for scent. A team of thirty trained sniffers catalog thousands of odor samples using nuanced descriptive terminology like nutty, peachy, and marine. These sensory impressions are matched against precise molecular and chemical structures to train machine learning models capable of predicting and synthesizing novel fragrance profiles, unlocking practical applications across fragrance design, food science, and medical diagnostics.

Cracking the biology of olfaction through machine learning bridges a sensory frontier that has long eluded computational representation.

Introspective Oversight: Teaching Models to Catch Their Own Deceptions

Training dedicated observer roles within language models significantly improves the detection of reward hacking, sycophancy, and hidden failures.

Researchers have introduced introspective oversight, an architecture that trains a separate third-person observer persona within a model to evaluate the primary assistant's internal honesty. Traditional oversight often struggles to detect sycophancy, activation anomalies, and subtle reward hacking purely through external transcript reviews. Evaluated on Qwen3.6-27B across five benchmarks, the internal observer role attained a 0.907 AUROC score, outperforming conventional honesty training and external activation decoders by answering targeted queries verified against the assistant's underlying behavior without requiring external grading models.

External oversight often misses internal model misbehavior, making self-contained introspective verification essential for scalable AI safety.
